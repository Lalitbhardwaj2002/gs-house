# Series

> A series $\sum a_n$ converges when its partial sums converge. For series of positive terms a set of tests decides convergence: comparison, ratio, root, Raabe, Cauchy condensation and the integral test; alternating series have Leibnitz's test. The same chapter gives the definite integral as a limit of a sum.

## Series of positive terms

! note | Series of positive terms
- **Necessary condition**: $\sum a_n$ convergent $\Rightarrow a_n \to 0$. (If $a_n \not\to 0$, the series is not convergent; but $a_n \to 0$ is not enough: $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges.)
- $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges; $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$, $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}$ converge (alternating).
- Sums and multiples of convergent series converge, but $\sum a_n b_n$ need not ($a_n = b_n = \frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n}}$ gives $\sum \frac{1}{n}$).
- **Geometric** $\sum ar^{n-1}$ ($a \neq 0$): converges iff $\lvert r \rvert < 1$; diverges for $r \geq 1$; oscillates finitely for $r = -1$ and infinitely for $r < -1$.
- **$p$-test**: $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges iff $p > 1$.
- **Comparison**: $a_n \leq kb_n$: $\sum b_n$ convergent $\Rightarrow \sum a_n$ convergent. **Limit comparison**: $\frac{a_n}{b_n} \to l \neq 0$ finite $\Rightarrow$ both behave alike.
- **Cauchy condensation**: for $a_n \downarrow 0$, $\sum a_n$ and $\sum 2^n a_{2^n}$ behave alike.
- **Root test**: $\lim a_n^{1/n} = l$; **D'Alembert ratio test**: $\lim \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = l$. In both: $l < 1$ converges, $l > 1$ diverges, $l = 1$ no conclusion.
- **Raabe** (when the ratio test fails): $\lim n\left(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} - 1\right) = l$: $l > 1$ converges, $l < 1$ diverges.
- **Logarithmic test**: $\lim n \log \frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} = l$; **De Morgan–Bertrand**: $\lim \left[n\left(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} - 1\right) - 1\right]$ $\log n = l$; same conclusions.
- **Leibnitz**: $\sum (-1)^{n+1} a_n$ converges if $a_n$ decreases to 0.
- Integral as limit of a sum: $\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{r=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n} f\left(\frac{r}{n}\right) = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$ ($f$ integrable, e.g. continuous, on $[0, 1]$). In general, if $r$ runs from $r_1$ to $r_2$, the limits of integration are $\lim \frac{r_1}{n}$ and $\lim \frac{r_2}{n}$ (e.g. $r = 1$ to $2n$ gives $\int_0^2$).

! check | Conditions added
The geometric series needs $a \neq 0$: for $a = 0$ every term is 0 and the series converges for every $r$. For $r \geq 1$ it diverges to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$ with the sign of $a$. The limit-of-a-sum formula needs $f$ integrable on $[0, 1]$ (continuous is enough). The comparison, condensation, root, ratio, Raabe, logarithmic and De Morgan–Bertrand tests are stated for series of **positive** terms, as the heading says; Leibnitz's test is the one for alternating series.

! addon | Absolute convergence and the integral test
$\sum \lvert a_n \rvert$ convergent $\Rightarrow \sum a_n$ convergent (absolute convergence). Convergent but not absolutely: conditional (e.g. $\sum \frac{(-1)^n}{n}$). Integral test: for $f \downarrow$ positive, $\sum f(n)$ and $\int_1^\infty f$ behave alike; $\sum_{n \geq 2} \frac{1}{n(\log n)^p}$ converges iff $p > 1$.

! check | A condition added
$\sum \frac{1}{n(\log n)^p}$ starts at $n = 2$, since $\log 1 = 0$; the integral test is then used on $[2, \infty)$.

! example | Question SR1 — standard
Test for convergence: (a) $\sum \frac{n+1}{n^3+5}$, (b) $\sum \frac{n!}{n^n}$, (c) $\sum \frac{2^n}{n^2}$.
**Solution.** (a) Compare with $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$: ratio $\to 1$, so **convergent**. (b) Ratio $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \to \frac{1}{e} < 1$: **convergent**. (c) $a_n \to \infty$ (or ratio $\to 2 > 1$): **divergent**.

! example | Question SR2 — standard
Evaluate $\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{r=1}^{n} \frac{n}{n^2 + r^2}$.
**Solution.** $= \lim \sum \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + (r/n)^2} = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \boxed{\frac{\pi}{4}}$.

! trick | Shortcut / time-saver — Which test?
Factorials or $n$th powers $\to$ ratio test; a whole term raised to the power $n \to$ root test; ratio gives $1 \to$ Raabe; rational function of $n \to$ compare with $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$, $p =$ (degree of denominator $-$ degree of numerator), converges iff $p > 1$.

## What it means

- **Partial sums**: $S_n = a_1 + \cdots + a_n$. $\sum a_n = S$ means $S_n \to S$. For a series of positive terms, $S_n$ is increasing, so the series either converges or diverges to $+\infty$; it cannot oscillate.
- **The harmonic series** $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges even though $\frac{1}{n} \to 0$: grouping $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} > \frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{5} + \cdots + \frac{1}{8} > \frac{1}{2}$, and so on, the sum exceeds any bound.
- **The tests in order of use**: first check $a_n \to 0$; then try comparison with a $p$-series or a geometric series; then ratio or root; if the ratio test gives 1, try Raabe, then the logarithmic or De Morgan–Bertrand test.
- **Rearranging** an absolutely convergent series does not change its sum; a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to give any sum (Riemann).

! example | Example 1 — the root test
Test $\sum \left(\frac{n}{2n + 1}\right)^n$.
**Solution.** $a_n^{1/n} = \frac{n}{2n + 1} \to \frac{1}{2} < 1$: **convergent**.

! example | Example 2 — Raabe's test
Test $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ by the ratio test and by Raabe's test.
**Solution.** $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2} \to 1$: the ratio test fails. $n\left(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} - 1\right) = n \cdot \frac{2n + 1}{n^2} \to 2 > 1$: **convergent** by Raabe.

! example | Example 3 — Leibnitz's test
Does $\sum \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{\log(n + 1)}$ converge? Absolutely?
**Solution.** $\frac{1}{\log(n+1)}$ decreases to 0, so the series **converges** by Leibnitz. But $\frac{1}{\log(n+1)} > \frac{1}{n + 1}$, and $\sum \frac{1}{n + 1}$ diverges, so it is **not absolutely** convergent: it converges conditionally.

! example | Example 4 — a limit of a sum
Find $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1^2 + 2^2 + \cdots + n^2}{n^3}$.
**Solution.** $\sum_{r=1}^{n} \frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{r}{n}\right)^2 \to \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \boxed{\frac{1}{3}}$.

! example | Example 5 — other limits of integration
Find
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{1}{n + 1} + \frac{1}{n + 2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{3n}\right).$$
**Solution.** The sum is $\sum_{r=1}^{2n} \frac{1}{n} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + r/n}$. The limits are $\lim \frac{1}{n} = 0$ and $\lim \frac{2n}{n} = 2$, so the limit is $\int_0^2 \frac{dx}{1 + x} = \boxed{\log 3}$.

! example | Example 6 — Cauchy condensation
Test $\sum_{n \geq 2} \frac{1}{n \log n}$.
**Solution.** The terms decrease to 0. Condensing, $2^n a_{2^n} = \frac{2^n}{2^n \cdot n\log 2} = \frac{1}{n \log 2}$, a multiple of the harmonic series. So the series **diverges**, although very slowly.

? Does $a_n \to 0$ guarantee that $\sum a_n$ converges? :: No; $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ diverges although $\frac{1}{n} \to 0$.
? For which $p$ does $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ converge? :: For $p > 1$.
? What does the ratio test say when the limit is 1? :: Nothing; try Raabe's test.
? When does an alternating series $\sum (-1)^{n+1} a_n$ converge by Leibnitz's test? :: When $a_n$ decreases to 0.

! exam | What the exam asks
- Convergence of given series by comparison, ratio, root, Raabe or condensation.
- Absolute and conditional convergence; geometric and $p$-series.
- Limits of sums evaluated as definite integrals.

! recap | One-minute revision
- $a_n \to 0$ is necessary, not sufficient; $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges iff $p > 1$.
- Ratio and root: $< 1$ converges, $> 1$ diverges, $= 1$ no conclusion; then Raabe ($> 1$ converges).
- $\lim \sum \frac{1}{n} f\left(\frac{r}{n}\right) = \int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$.
