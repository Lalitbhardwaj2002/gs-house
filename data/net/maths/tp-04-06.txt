# Subgroups

> A subgroup is a subset that is itself a group under the same operation. The one-step and two-step tests decide it quickly, and the TGT syllabus also asks for the subgroup generated by a subset.

## Subgroups

! note | Subgroups
- $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** if it is a group under the same operation.
- **Tests**: $H \leq G \iff (a, b \in H \Rightarrow ab \in H$ and $a^{-1} \in H) \iff (a, b \in H \Rightarrow ab^{-1} \in H) \iff HH^{-1} = H$. A finite non-empty subset closed under the operation is a subgroup.
- The intersection of subgroups is a subgroup. A union need not be: $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 3\mathbb{Z}$ is not ($2 + 3 = 5$), but $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 4\mathbb{Z} = 2\mathbb{Z}$ is.
- Example: $H = \{1, i\}$ is not a subgroup of $\{1, -1, i, -i\}$ since $i^{-1} = -i \notin H$.
- If $o(H)$ and $o(K)$ are coprime, $H \cap K = \{e\}$.

! check | The tests need $H$ non-empty
All three tests are for a **non-empty** subset $H$: the empty set passes them trivially but is not a subgroup. In practice, first check that $e \in H$.

## What it means

- Every group has the two **trivial** (improper) subgroups $\{e\}$ and $G$; the others are **proper** (non-trivial) subgroups. Some books call every subgroup other than $G$ proper, so $\{e\}$ is then proper too.
- A subgroup has the **same identity** as the group, and the inverse of an element is the same in both.
- **In additive notation** the one-step test reads: $a, b \in H \Rightarrow a - b \in H$. With it one shows that the subgroups of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ are exactly the sets $n\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -n, 0, n, 2n, \dots\}$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$: a subgroup $H \neq \{0\}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for its least positive element $n$.
- **Union of two subgroups** is a subgroup only when one contains the other.
- **Why the coprime fact holds**: $H \cap K$ is a subgroup of both, so its order divides both $o(H)$ and $o(K)$ (Lagrange), hence it is 1.

## The subgroup generated by a subset

- For a subset $S$ of $G$, the **subgroup generated by $S$**, $\langle S \rangle$, is the smallest subgroup containing $S$: the intersection of all subgroups that contain $S$. Its elements are all finite products of elements of $S$ and their inverses (and $\langle \emptyset \rangle = \{e\}$).
- For one element, $\langle a \rangle = \{a^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$.
- In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$: $\langle a, b \rangle = \gcd(a, b)\,\mathbb{Z}$. For example $\langle 4, 6 \rangle = 2\mathbb{Z}$, since $2 = 4 \cdot (-1) + 6 \cdot 1$.
- In a non-abelian group Lagrange's theorem (4.7) saves work. In $S_3$, $\langle (1\,2\,3) \rangle = \{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$ has order 3. But $\langle (1\,2), (1\,2\,3) \rangle$ contains subgroups of orders 2 and 3, so its order is a multiple of 6: it is the whole of $S_3$.

! example | Example 1 — a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^*$
Is $H = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^* : x > 0\}$ a subgroup of $(\mathbb{R}^*, \times)$? Is $K = \{x : x < 0\}$?
**Solution.** $H$: non-empty, and $a, b > 0 \Rightarrow ab^{-1} > 0$, so **yes**. $K$: $(-1)(-1) = 1 \notin K$ and $1 \notin K$, so **no**.

! example | Example 2 — subgroups of a matrix group
Show that $H = \left\{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} : x \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$ is a subgroup of the invertible $2 \times 2$ real matrices under multiplication.
**Solution.** $I \in H$ ($x = 0$). For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & y \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $AB^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x - y \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in H$. So $H$ is a subgroup.

! example | Example 3 — the generated subgroup
Find $\langle 6, 10 \rangle$ in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ and $\langle 3 \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$.
**Solution.** $\langle 6, 10 \rangle = \gcd(6, 10)\,\mathbb{Z} = \boxed{2\mathbb{Z}}$. In $\mathbb{Z}_{12}$, $\langle 3 \rangle = \boxed{\{0, 3, 6, 9\}}$, a subgroup of order 4.

? What is the one-step subgroup test? :: A non-empty $H$ is a subgroup iff $a, b \in H \Rightarrow ab^{-1} \in H$.
? Is the union of two subgroups always a subgroup? :: No; only when one contains the other (e.g. $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 3\mathbb{Z}$ is not).
? What are the subgroups of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$? :: Exactly the sets $n\mathbb{Z}$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$
? What is $\langle 4, 6 \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Z}$? :: $2\mathbb{Z}$, generated by $\gcd(4, 6) = 2$.

! exam | What the exam asks
- Whether a given subset is a subgroup; finite subsets closed under the operation.
- Intersections and unions of subgroups.
- The subgroup generated by a subset, especially in $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

! recap | One-minute revision
- Non-empty $H$: $ab^{-1} \in H$ for all $a, b \in H$; finite and closed is enough.
- Intersection: always a subgroup; union: only if one contains the other.
- $\langle S \rangle$ = smallest subgroup containing $S$; $\langle a, b \rangle = \gcd(a, b)\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.
