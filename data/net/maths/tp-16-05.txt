# Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution

> Repeat an experiment with two outcomes $n$ times, independently, with the same chance $p$ of success: the number of successes follows the binomial distribution $B(n, p)$, with mean $np$ and variance $npq$. Most exam questions give two of $n$, $p$, mean and variance and ask for the rest.

## The binomial distribution

! note | Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution
- **Bernoulli trials**: finite number $n$ of independent trials, each with only two outcomes (success/failure), $p = P(\text{success})$ constant, $q = 1 - p$.
- **Binomial distribution** $B(n, p)$: $P(X = r) = {}^nC_rp^rq^{n-r}$, $r = 0, 1, \ldots, n$ (these are the terms of $(q + p)^n$).
- **Mean** $= np$, **variance** $= npq$, **SD** $= \sqrt{npq}$. Since $q < 1$, variance $<$ mean.
^ data/net/maths/fig/prob-binomial.svg | $B(4, \frac{1}{2})$: four coins, number of heads; $P(X = r) = \frac{1}{16}, \frac{4}{16}, \frac{6}{16}, \frac{4}{16}, \frac{1}{16}$

! check | A condition added
"Variance $<$ mean" needs $0 < p$ (and $n \geq 1$); for $p = 0$ both are $0$.

! example | Question BI1 — RS Aggarwal Ex.13 (CBSE 2004C)
Find the binomial distribution whose mean is 12 and variance 3.
**Solution.** $np = 12$, $npq = 3 \Rightarrow q = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}$, $p = \frac{3}{4}$, $n = \frac{12}{3/4} = 16$.
$$\begin{aligned} &P(X = r) \\ &= \boxed{{}^{16}C_r\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^r\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\right)^{16-r}}. \end{aligned}$$

! example | Question BI2 — RS Aggarwal Ex.14 (CBSE 2004)
The sum of the mean and variance of a binomial distribution for 5 trials is 1.8. Find the distribution.
**Solution.** $5p + 5pq = 1.8 \Rightarrow p + p(1 - p) = 0.36 \Rightarrow p^2 - 2p + 0.36 = 0 \Rightarrow 25p^2 - 50p + 9 = 0 \Rightarrow (5p - 1)(5p - 9) = 0$. $p \leq 1 \Rightarrow p = \frac{1}{5}$, $q = \frac{4}{5}$: $P(X = r) = \boxed{{}^5C_r(0.2)^r(0.8)^{5-r}}$.

! example | Question BI3 — RS Aggarwal Ex.15
The sum and the product of the mean and variance of a binomial distribution are 24 and 128. Find the distribution.
**Solution.** $np(1 + q) = 24$ and $n^2p^2q = 128$. Squaring the first: $n^2p^2(1 + q)^2 = 576$. Dividing: $\frac{(1 + q)^2}{q} = \frac{576}{128} = \frac{9}{2} \Rightarrow 2q^2 - 5q + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow q = \frac{1}{2}$ ($q \neq 2$). $p = \frac{1}{2}$; $n \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 24 \Rightarrow n = 32$.
$$P(X = r) = \boxed{{}^{32}C_r\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{32}}.$$

! example | Question BI4 — RS Aggarwal Ex.17–19
(a) A die is tossed thrice; an even number is a success. Find the variance. (b) A die is rolled 20 times; a number greater than 4 is a success. Find mean and variance. (c) A die is tossed 180 times. Find the expected number of times the face 5 appears, and the SD.
**Solution.** (a) $n = 3$, $p = q = \frac{1}{2}$: $npq = \boxed{\frac{3}{4}}$.
(b) $p = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$: mean $= \frac{20}{3} \approx \boxed{6.67}$, variance $= 20 \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{40}{9} \approx \boxed{4.44}$.
(c) $p = \frac{1}{6}$: $\mu = 180 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \boxed{30}$, $\sigma^2 = 30 \cdot \frac{5}{6} = 25$, $\sigma = \boxed{5}$.

! example | Question BI5 — RS Aggarwal Ex.16 and Exercise Q32
Prove that for a binomial distribution mean $>$ variance. Hence bring out the fallacy in "the mean of a binomial distribution is 6 and its variance is 9".
**Solution.** Mean $-$ variance $= np - npq = np(1 - q) = np^2 > 0$. So mean $>$ variance. Here variance $9 >$ mean $6$, i.e. $q = \frac{9}{6} = \frac{3}{2} > 1$, impossible. **Fallacy shown.**

! example | Question BI6 — Exercise 4, Q1–2
(a) A coin is tossed 6 times. Find the probability of at least 3 heads. (b) A coin is tossed 5 times. Find the probability that a head appears an odd number of times.
**Solution.** (a)
$$\begin{aligned} &P(X \geq 3) \\ &= \frac{{}^6C_3 + {}^6C_4 + {}^6C_5 + {}^6C_6}{2^6} \\ &= \frac{20 + 15 + 6 + 1}{64} = \boxed{\frac{21}{32}}. \end{aligned}$$
(b) $\frac{{}^5C_1 + {}^5C_3 + {}^5C_5}{2^5} = \frac{5 + 10 + 1}{32} = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}}$.

! trick | Shortcut / time-saver
Fair coin: $P(X = r) = \frac{{}^nC_r}{2^n}$; odd and even numbers of heads are equally likely ($\frac{1}{2}$). Given mean and variance: $q = \frac{\text{variance}}{\text{mean}}$ first. Mode of $B(n, p)$: integer part of $(n + 1)p$ (if $(n + 1)p$ is an integer, both $(n + 1)p$ and $(n + 1)p - 1$ are modes).

! check | Conditions added
"Odd and even numbers of heads are equally likely" is for a fair coin and $n \geq 1$. The mode rule needs $0 < p < 1$: for $p = 1$ the only value is $n$, for $p = 0$ it is $0$.

## Poisson and normal distributions

! addon | Poisson and normal distributions; rules of expectation
- $E(aX + b) = aE(X) + b$, $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\text{Var}(X)$; independent $X, Y$: $E(XY) = E(X)E(Y)$, $\text{Var}(X \pm Y) = \text{Var}X + \text{Var}Y$.
- **Poisson** (limit of binomial, $n$ large, $p$ small, $np = \lambda$): $P(X = r) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^r}{r!}$; mean $=$ variance $= \lambda$.
- **Normal**: bell-shaped, symmetric about the mean; mean $=$ median $=$ mode; about $68\%$, $95\%$, $99.7\%$ of values lie within $1\sigma$, $2\sigma$, $3\sigma$ of the mean.
^ data/net/maths/fig/prob-normal.svg | the normal curve; about $68\%$ of the values lie between $\mu - \sigma$ and $\mu + \sigma$

! check | A condition added
The rules $E(XY) = E(X)E(Y)$ and $\text{Var}(X \pm Y) = \text{Var}X + \text{Var}Y$ need independence (uncorrelated is enough); $E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)$ always holds.

## What it means

- **Where ${}^nC_rp^rq^{n-r}$ comes from**: one particular order of $r$ successes and $n - r$ failures has probability $p^rq^{n-r}$, and there are ${}^nC_r$ orders.
- **Mean $np$**: each trial contributes $p$ successes on average; the variance $npq$ adds the variances $pq$ of the $n$ independent trials.
- **Poisson** counts rare events (misprints on a page, calls in a minute) when only the average rate $\lambda$ is known.

! example | Example 1 — at least one success
A die is thrown 4 times. Find the probability of at least one six.
**Solution.** $1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4 = 1 - \frac{625}{1296} = \boxed{\frac{671}{1296}} \approx 0.52$.

! example | Example 2 — the mode
Find the most likely number of heads when a fair coin is tossed $9$ times.
**Solution.** $(n + 1)p = 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 5$, an integer, so $\boxed{4 \text{ and } 5}$ are both modes (${}^9C_4 = {}^9C_5 = 126$).

! example | Example 3 — Poisson
Misprints occur at an average rate of $2$ per page. Find the probability that a page has no misprint.
**Solution.** $\lambda = 2$: $P(X = 0) = e^{-2} \approx \boxed{0.135}$.

? Binomial probability of $r$ successes? :: ${}^nC_rp^rq^{n-r}$.
? Mean and variance of $B(n, p)$? :: $np$ and $npq$.
? Mean and variance of a Poisson distribution? :: Both $\lambda$.
? Fraction of a normal population within $2\sigma$ of the mean? :: About $95\%$.

! exam | What the exam asks
- The binomial distribution from its mean and variance (or their sum or product).
- At least / at most / exactly $r$ successes.
- Mean $>$ variance; the mode; the Poisson limit; the normal curve's $68$–$95$–$99.7$ rule.

! recap | One-minute revision
- $P(X = r) = {}^nC_rp^rq^{n-r}$; mean $np$, variance $npq$, $q = \frac{\text{variance}}{\text{mean}}$.
- Mode: integer part of $(n + 1)p$.
- Poisson: $\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^r}{r!}$, mean $=$ variance $= \lambda$.
