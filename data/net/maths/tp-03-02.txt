# Linear dependence, basis, dimension

> A basis is a smallest set of vectors that still builds the whole space; the number of vectors in it is the dimension. Questions ask whether given vectors are independent, whether they form a basis, and what the dimension of a space of matrices or polynomials is.

## Independence, basis and dimension

! addon | Linear dependence, basis, dimension
- $v_1, \dots, v_k$ are **linearly independent** if $\sum a_i v_i = 0 \Rightarrow$ all $a_i = 0$. Any set containing $0$ is LD. Two vectors are LD iff one is a scalar multiple of the other.
- In $\mathbb{R}^n$: $n$ vectors are LI iff the determinant of the matrix formed by them is non-zero. More than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are always LD.
- **Basis**: LI spanning set; **dimension** = number of vectors in a basis.
- In an $n$-dimensional space any $n$ LI vectors form a basis; any LI set extends to a basis.
- Dimensions to remember: symmetric $n \times n$ matrices $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$; skew-symmetric $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$; upper triangular $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$; trace-zero matrices $n^2 - 1$; diagonal $n$.

! example | Example
Are $(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$ a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$?
**Solution.**
$$\begin{aligned} & \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \\ &= 1(1) - 1(0 - 1) + 0 = 2 \neq 0. \end{aligned}$$
Yes: the three vectors are independent, and three independent vectors in the 3-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ form a basis.

## What it means

- **LD** stands for linearly dependent and **LI** for linearly independent. Vectors are dependent exactly when one of them is a combination of the others.
- The **span** of a set is the set of all its linear combinations; a set **spans** $V$ if its span is $V$.
- Every vector of $V$ is written in **exactly one** way as a combination of a basis; the coefficients are its **coordinates**.
- In an $n$-dimensional space: fewer than $n$ vectors cannot span; more than $n$ cannot be independent; exactly $n$ vectors span if and only if they are independent.
- **Standard bases**: $e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0), \dots, e_n$ for $\mathbb{R}^n$; $1, x, \dots, x^n$ for $P_n$; the matrices $E_{ij}$ (a 1 in place $(i, j)$, 0 elsewhere) for $M_{m \times n}$.
- The dimension of the span of some vectors is the **rank** of the matrix whose rows are those vectors.
- **Why the matrix dimensions hold**: count the entries that can be chosen freely. A symmetric matrix is fixed by its diagonal and the entries above it, $n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$; a skew-symmetric one by the entries above the diagonal alone, $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$; a trace-zero matrix by all entries except one diagonal entry, $n^2 - 1$.
- Polynomials and matrices are tested like vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$: write each one as its list of coefficients (or entries) and use a determinant or the rank.

! example | Example 1 — testing for dependence
Are $(1, 2, 3)$, $(2, 5, 7)$, $(1, 3, 4)$ linearly independent?
**Solution.** $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 7 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 1(20 - 21) - 2(8 - 7) + 3(6 - 5) = -1 - 2 + 3 = 0$. So they are **dependent**; indeed $(2, 5, 7) - (1, 2, 3) = (1, 3, 4)$.

! example | Example 2 — the dimension of a span
Find the dimension of the span of $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 2)$, $(2, 1, 3)$.
**Solution.** $(1, 1, 2) = (1, 0, 1) + (0, 1, 1)$ and $(2, 1, 3) = 2(1, 0, 1) + (0, 1, 1)$, while the first two are independent. The dimension is $\boxed{2}$.

! example | Example 3 — coordinates
Find the coordinates of $(3, 2, 1)$ in the basis $(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$.
**Solution.** $a(1, 1, 0) + b(0, 1, 1) + c(1, 0, 1) = (3, 2, 1)$ gives $a + c = 3$, $a + b = 2$, $b + c = 1$. Adding, $a + b + c = 3$, so $b = 0$, $c = 1$, $a = 2$: coordinates $\boxed{(2, 0, 1)}$.

! example | Example 4 — a basis of polynomials
Do $1 + x$, $x + x^2$, $1 + x^2$ form a basis of $P_2$?
**Solution.** Their coefficient lists in the basis $1, x, x^2$ are $(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$, the vectors of the first example, with determinant $2 \neq 0$. So they are independent, and three independent vectors in the 3-dimensional $P_2$ form a basis: **yes**.

? Is a set containing the zero vector independent? :: No; any set containing 0 is dependent.
? Can 4 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ be independent? :: No; more than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are always dependent.
? Dimension of the space of $3 \times 3$ symmetric matrices? :: $\frac{3 \cdot 4}{2} = 6$.
? Dimension of the space of $n \times n$ matrices with trace 0? :: $n^2 - 1$.

! exam | What the exam asks
- Whether given vectors (or polynomials, or matrices) are independent or form a basis.
- Dimensions of standard subspaces of matrices and polynomials.
- The dimension of the span of a given set.

! recap | One-minute revision
- LI: only the trivial combination gives 0; $n$ vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are LI iff their determinant is non-zero.
- Basis = independent + spanning; every basis has $\dim V$ vectors.
- Symmetric $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$, skew $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$, trace-zero $n^2 - 1$, diagonal $n$.
