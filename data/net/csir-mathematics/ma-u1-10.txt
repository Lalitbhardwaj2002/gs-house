# Functions of several variables

> For functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$ the single idea "derivative" splits into several: partial derivatives, directional derivatives and the total derivative, which is a linear map. The inverse and implicit function theorems say when a smooth map can be undone locally.

## Limits and continuity

- $\lim_{(x,y) \to (a,b)} f(x,y) = l$ means $f$ is close to $l$ whenever $(x,y)$ is close to $(a,b)$, along **every** path.
- To show that a limit does **not** exist, find two paths with different limits, for example $y = mx$ with a limit depending on $m$, or $y = x^2$.
- To show that it **does** exist, bound $\lvert f(x,y) - l \rvert$ by something that tends to 0, or use polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ and a bound independent of $\theta$.
- The repeated limits $\lim_x \lim_y f$ and $\lim_y \lim_x f$ may exist and be equal while the double limit does not exist.

| Function, with $f(0,0) = 0$ | At the origin |
| $\frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ | No limit: along $y = mx$ the value is $\frac{m}{1+m^2}$; both partial derivatives exist |
| $\frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$ | No limit (path $y = x^2$), yet every directional derivative exists |
| $\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$ | Continuous, partial derivatives exist, not differentiable |
| $\frac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2}$ | Continuous, all directional derivatives exist, not differentiable |
| $(x^2 + y^2) \sin\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$ | Differentiable, but the partial derivatives are not continuous |

## Derivatives

- **Partial derivatives** $f_x, f_y$: derivatives along the coordinate axes.
- **Directional derivative** along a unit vector $u$: $D_u f(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(a + tu) - f(a)}{t}$.
- $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is **differentiable** at $a$ if there is a linear map $T$ with $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\lVert f(a+h) - f(a) - T h \rVert}{\lVert h \rVert} = 0$. $T = Df(a)$ is the **total derivative**; its matrix is the **Jacobian matrix** $\left( \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j} \right)$.

! remember | How the notions are related
- Continuous partial derivatives ($C^1$) implies differentiable. Differentiable implies continuous, and implies all directional derivatives exist with $D_u f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u$.
- No arrow reverses: partial derivatives, or even all directional derivatives, may exist at a point where $f$ is not continuous.
- The derivative of a linear map $T$ is $T$ itself at every point. For $f(x) = \langle x, x \rangle$, $Df(a)h = 2\langle a, h \rangle$.
- **Chain rule**: $D(g \circ f)(a) = Dg(f(a)) \, Df(a)$, a product of Jacobian matrices.
- **Mixed partials** (Schwarz, Clairaut): if $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ exist near a point and are continuous at it, they are equal there. Without continuity they can differ: $f = \frac{xy(x^2 - y^2)}{x^2 + y^2}$, $f(0,0) = 0$, has mixed partials $-1$ and $1$ at the origin.
- **Euler's theorem**: if a differentiable $f$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, then $x f_x + y f_y = n f$.

## Maxima and minima

- At an interior extremum of a differentiable $f$, $\nabla f = 0$ (a critical point).
- For two variables put $r = f_{xx}$, $s = f_{xy}$, $t = f_{yy}$ at a critical point:

| Condition | Conclusion |
| $rt - s^2 > 0$ and $r > 0$ | Local minimum |
| $rt - s^2 > 0$ and $r < 0$ | Local maximum |
| $rt - s^2 < 0$ | Saddle point |
| $rt - s^2 = 0$ | The test fails |

- In $n$ variables (for $C^2$ functions at a critical point): the Hessian positive definite gives a minimum, negative definite a maximum, indefinite a saddle.
- **Lagrange multipliers**: to optimise $f$ subject to $g = 0$, solve $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ together with $g = 0$ (valid at points where $\nabla g \ne 0$).

## Inverse and implicit function theorems

- **Inverse function theorem**: if $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is $C^1$ and $Df(a)$ is invertible (Jacobian determinant not zero), then $f$ is one-one near $a$, maps a neighbourhood of $a$ onto a neighbourhood of $f(a)$, and the local inverse is $C^1$ with $D(f^{-1})(f(a)) = [Df(a)]^{-1}$.
- A non-zero Jacobian everywhere gives local, not global, invertibility: $f(x,y) = (e^x \cos y, e^x \sin y)$ has Jacobian $e^{2x} \ne 0$ but is not one-one.
- **Implicit function theorem**: if $F(x,y)$ is $C^1$, $F(a,b) = 0$ and $F_y(a,b) \ne 0$, then near $(a,b)$ the equation $F = 0$ defines $y = g(x)$ with $g$ of class $C^1$ and $g'(x) = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}$.

? How do you show that a limit in two variables does not exist? :: Find two paths to the point along which the function has different limits.
? Does the existence of all directional derivatives imply continuity? :: No: $\frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$ at the origin.
? What is the derivative of a linear map $T$? :: $T$ itself, at every point.
? What does the inverse function theorem require? :: A $C^1$ map whose derivative at the point is invertible.

! recap | One-minute revision
- $C^1$ implies differentiable implies continuous with all directional derivatives; no converses.
- Second derivative test: sign of $rt - s^2$, then sign of $r$.
- Invertible derivative gives a local inverse; $F_y \ne 0$ lets $F = 0$ be solved for $y$.
