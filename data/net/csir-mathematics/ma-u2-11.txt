# Rings, ideals and factorisation domains

> A ring has two operations. Its ideals play the part that normal subgroups play for groups, and the quality of a quotient is read from the ideal: prime ideals give integral domains, maximal ideals give fields.

## Rings and special elements

- A **ring** $(R, +, \cdot)$: an abelian group under $+$, with an associative multiplication that distributes over addition. Rings here are commutative with 1 unless stated.
- A **unit** has a multiplicative inverse. A **zero divisor** is a non-zero $a$ with $ab = 0$ for some non-zero $b$. $a$ is **idempotent** if $a^2 = a$ and **nilpotent** if $a^n = 0$ for some $n$.
- An **integral domain** has no zero divisors. A **field** is a ring in which every non-zero element is a unit. Every field is an integral domain, and every **finite** integral domain is a field.
- The **characteristic** is the least $n \ge 1$ with $n \cdot 1 = 0$ (0 if there is none). The characteristic of an integral domain is 0 or a prime.
- In a ring of prime characteristic $p$: $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$.

| In $\mathbb{Z}_n$ | Description | Number |
| Units | $a$ with $\gcd(a,n) = 1$ | $\varphi(n)$ |
| Zero divisors | Non-zero $a$ with $\gcd(a,n) > 1$ | $n - \varphi(n) - 1$ |
| Idempotents | Solutions of $a^2 = a$ | $2^k$, $k$ = number of distinct primes dividing $n$ |
| Nilpotents | Multiples of the product of the distinct primes of $n$ | $\frac{n}{p_1 \cdots p_k}$ |
| Ideals | One for each divisor of $n$ | $\tau(n)$ |
| Maximal (= prime) ideals | $p\mathbb{Z}_n$, $p$ a prime dividing $n$ | $k$ |

- $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime; it is an integral domain exactly then.
- Units of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: $\pm 1, \pm i$. Units of $F[x]$: the non-zero constants. Units of $M_n(F)$: matrices with non-zero determinant.

## Ideals and quotient rings

- An **ideal** $I$ is an additive subgroup with $ra \in I$ for all $r \in R$, $a \in I$. The quotient $R/I$ is a ring.
- A commutative ring with 1 is a field if and only if its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $R$.
- Sum $I + J$, product $IJ$ and intersection $I \cap J$ are ideals, and $IJ \subseteq I \cap J$. In $\mathbb{Z}$: $m\mathbb{Z} + n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ and $m\mathbb{Z} \cap n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$.

! remember | Prime and maximal ideals
- $P$ is **prime** if $ab \in P$ implies $a \in P$ or $b \in P$. $R/P$ is an integral domain if and only if $P$ is prime.
- $M$ is **maximal** if no ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$. $R/M$ is a field if and only if $M$ is maximal.
- Every maximal ideal is prime. The converse fails: $(0)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, and $(x)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
- In a **PID** every non-zero prime ideal is maximal. In a finite ring, prime and maximal ideals coincide.
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: the prime ideals are $(0)$ and $(p)$; the maximal ones are $(p)$.
- In $F[x]$, $F$ a field: for $f \ne 0$, $(f)$ is maximal if and only if $f$ is irreducible, and then $F[x]/(f)$ is a field.
- In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: $(x)$ is prime, not maximal; $(2, x)$ is maximal and not principal.
- In $C[0,1]$: the maximal ideals are $M_c = \{f : f(c) = 0\}$ for $c \in [0,1]$.

## Ring homomorphisms

- A ring homomorphism preserves both operations (it need not send 1 to 1 unless this is asked for, and the counts below do not ask it); its kernel is an ideal, and $R/\ker\varphi \cong \varphi(R)$.
- The number of ring homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}_m \to \mathbb{Z}_n$ equals the number of idempotents $e$ of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ with $me = 0$. From $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Z}_n$ there are as many as idempotents of $\mathbb{Z}_n$.
- The only ring homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$ are 0 and the identity. A homomorphism from a field to a ring is one-one or zero.
- **Chinese remainder theorem**: if $I + J = R$, then $R/(I \cap J) \cong R/I \times R/J$.

## Factorisation in integral domains

- In an integral domain, $p$ is **irreducible** if it is not a unit and $p = ab$ forces $a$ or $b$ to be a unit; $p$ is **prime** if $p \mid ab$ forces $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. Prime implies irreducible; the converse holds in a UFD.

| Class | Definition | Examples |
| Euclidean domain (ED) | Has a division algorithm | $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, any field |
| Principal ideal domain (PID) | Every ideal is generated by one element | Every ED; $\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\right]$ is a PID that is not an ED |
| Unique factorisation domain (UFD) | Unique factorisation into irreducibles | Every PID; $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $F[x,y]$ are UFDs that are not PIDs |
| Integral domain | No zero divisors | $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$ |

- Field $\subset$ ED $\subset$ PID $\subset$ UFD $\subset$ integral domain, each inclusion strict.
- $R[x]$ is a UFD when $R$ is a UFD (**Gauss**); for an integral domain $R$, $R[x]$ is a PID if and only if $R$ is a field.
- In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: a prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}$ stays prime; $p \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$ and $p = 2$ are sums of two squares, $p = a^2 + b^2 = (a + bi)(a - bi)$, so they are not prime there ($2 = (1 + i)(1 - i)$).
- $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$, $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, are also Euclidean. $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ is not a UFD: $4 = 2 \cdot 2 = (1 + \sqrt{-3})(1 - \sqrt{-3})$.

? When is $R/I$ a field? :: Exactly when $I$ is a maximal ideal.
? How many idempotents are there in $\mathbb{Z}_{30}$? :: $2^3 = 8$.
? Give a UFD that is not a PID. :: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, where $(2, x)$ is not principal.
? Is every finite integral domain a field? :: Yes.

- Whether a given ideal is prime or maximal, and what the quotient ring is.
- Placing a ring in the chain ED, PID, UFD; counting ring homomorphisms.

! recap | One-minute revision
- $R/P$ integral domain: $P$ prime. $R/M$ field: $M$ maximal.
- $\mathbb{Z}_n$: $\varphi(n)$ units, $2^k$ idempotents, $\tau(n)$ ideals.
- ED inside PID inside UFD; $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD but not a PID; $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.
