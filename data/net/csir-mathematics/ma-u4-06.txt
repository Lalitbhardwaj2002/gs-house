# Nonparametric tests and Bayesian inference

> Nonparametric tests use signs and ranks, so they need hardly any assumption about the shape of the distribution. Bayesian inference treats the parameter itself as random and updates a prior distribution by the data.

## Nonparametric methods

- A test is **distribution-free** if the null distribution of its statistic is the same for every continuous parent distribution.
- Such tests need only continuity (sometimes symmetry), are little affected by outliers, and can be used on ordinal data. When the normal model really holds they are somewhat less powerful than the $t$ test.
- The **empirical distribution function** $F_n(x)$ is the proportion of observations $\le x$. $nF_n(x) \sim$ Binomial$(n, F(x))$, so $E[F_n(x)] = F(x)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(F_n(x)) = \frac{F(x)(1 - F(x))}{n}$. **Glivenko-Cantelli**: $\sup_x \lvert F_n(x) - F(x) \rvert \to 0$ almost surely.

## One-sample tests

| Test | Hypothesis | Statistic and null distribution |
| Sign test | Median $= m_0$ | Number of observations above $m_0$; Binomial$\left(n, \frac{1}{2}\right)$ |
| Wilcoxon signed-rank | Median $= m_0$, distribution symmetric | $T^+$, the sum of ranks of $\lvert x_i - m_0 \rvert$ for positive differences; mean $\frac{n(n + 1)}{4}$, variance $\frac{n(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{24}$ |
| Kolmogorov-Smirnov | $F = F_0$, fully specified | $D_n = \sup_x \lvert F_n(x) - F_0(x) \rvert$ |
| Runs test | The sequence is random | Number of runs $R$; mean $\frac{2n_1n_2}{n_1 + n_2} + 1$ |
| Chi-square goodness of fit | $F = F_0$, grouped data | $\sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}$ |

- The variance of the number of runs is $\frac{2n_1n_2(2n_1n_2 - n_1 - n_2)}{(n_1 + n_2)^2(n_1 + n_2 - 1)}$. Too few or too many runs both speak against randomness.
- For paired data apply the sign test or the signed-rank test to the differences.
- The sign test uses only the signs; the signed-rank test also uses the sizes, and so is usually more powerful when the symmetry assumption holds. Not always: for the Laplace distribution the sign test is the more efficient (relative efficiency $\frac{4}{3}$).

## Two-sample tests

| Test | Hypothesis | Statistic |
| Mann-Whitney $U$ (Wilcoxon rank-sum) | The two distributions are the same, against a shift | $U$ = number of pairs $(X_i, Y_j)$ with $X_i > Y_j$ |
| Median test | Common median | Number of first-sample values above the pooled median; hypergeometric |
| Wald-Wolfowitz runs test | The two distributions are the same | Number of runs in the pooled ordered sample; few runs lead to rejection |
| Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample | The two distributions are the same | $\sup_x \lvert F_m(x) - G_n(x) \rvert$ |
| Kruskal-Wallis | $k$ distributions are the same | Rank analogue of one-way analysis of variance; approximately $\chi^2_{k-1}$ |

! remember | Mann-Whitney and Wilcoxon
- With sample sizes $m$ and $n$: $E[U] = \frac{mn}{2}$ and $\operatorname{Var}(U) = \frac{mn(m + n + 1)}{12}$ under $H_0$.
- If $W$ is the sum of the ranks of the first sample in the pooled sample, then $U = W - \frac{m(m + 1)}{2}$ and $E[W] = \frac{m(m + n + 1)}{2}$.
- $U$ ranges from $0$ to $mn$ and its null distribution is symmetric about $\frac{mn}{2}$.
- For large samples both are approximately normal.
- **Efficiency relative to the $t$ test** (Pitman): for normal data the sign test has efficiency $\frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.64$ and the Wilcoxon tests $\frac{3}{\pi} \approx 0.955$; for the Wilcoxon tests it never falls below $0.864$ and can exceed 1 for heavy-tailed data.

## Rank correlation and tests of independence

- **Spearman's coefficient** is the ordinary correlation coefficient of the ranks. Without ties, with $d_i$ the difference of ranks,

$$r_s = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}$$

- $\sum d_i = 0$; $r_s = 1$ for identical rankings and $-1$ for opposite rankings, when $\sum d_i^2 = \frac{n(n^2 - 1)}{3}$. For ties, add $\frac{m^3 - m}{12}$ to $\sum d_i^2$ for each group of $m$ tied ranks.
- Under independence $E[r_s] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(r_s) = \frac{1}{n - 1}$.
- **Kendall's tau**: $\tau = \frac{C - D}{\binom{n}{2}}$, where $C$ and $D$ count concordant and discordant pairs. Under independence $E[\tau] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\tau) = \frac{2(2n + 5)}{9n(n - 1)}$.
- Both lie in $[-1, 1]$ and are unchanged by strictly increasing transformations of either variable.
- For categorical data, independence is tested by the chi-square statistic of the contingency table with $(r - 1)(c - 1)$ degrees of freedom.

## Elementary Bayesian inference

- The parameter has a **prior** density $\pi(\theta)$. After observing $x$ the **posterior** is

$$\pi(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f(x \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\int f(x \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta} \propto \text{likelihood} \times \text{prior}$$

- A family of priors is **conjugate** to a model if the posterior belongs to the same family.

| Model | Prior | Posterior |
| Binomial$(n, p)$, $x$ successes | Beta$(a, b)$ | Beta$(a + x, b + n - x)$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$, $n$ observations with total $s$ | Gamma$(a, b)$ ($b$ the rate) | Gamma$(a + s, b + n)$ |
| Exponential with rate $\lambda$, $n$ observations with total $s$ | Gamma$(a, b)$ | Gamma$(a + n, b + s)$ |
| $N(\theta, \sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2$ known, $n$ observations | $N(\mu_0, \tau^2)$ | Normal with precision $\frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}$ and mean $\frac{\mu_0/\tau^2 + n\bar{x}/\sigma^2}{1/\tau^2 + n/\sigma^2}$ |

! remember | Bayes estimators
- Under **squared error loss** the Bayes estimator is the posterior **mean**; under **absolute error loss** the posterior **median**; under 0-1 loss the posterior **mode**.
- Binomial with a uniform prior, Beta$(1, 1)$: the posterior mean is $\frac{x + 1}{n + 2}$. With a Beta$(a, b)$ prior it is $\frac{a + x}{a + b + n}$, a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample proportion.
- In the normal model the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and $\bar{x}$, with weights proportional to the precisions; as $n$ grows the data dominate.
- A Bayes estimator that is unique is **admissible**. A Bayes estimator with constant risk is **minimax**.
- A **credible interval** has posterior probability $1 - \alpha$ of containing $\theta$; for a unimodal posterior the **highest posterior density** interval is the shortest. This is a statement about $\theta$ given the data, unlike a confidence interval.
- **Non-informative priors**: uniform, or **Jeffreys' prior** proportional to $\sqrt{I(\theta)}$, which for the Bernoulli model is Beta$\left(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right)$. An improper prior can still give a proper posterior.
- Posterior odds $=$ prior odds $\times$ **Bayes factor**.

? Null distribution of the sign test statistic? :: Binomial$\left(n, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.
? Mean of the Mann-Whitney $U$ under the null hypothesis? :: $\frac{mn}{2}$.
? Bayes estimator under squared error loss? :: The posterior mean.
? Posterior for a binomial proportion with a Beta$(a, b)$ prior and $x$ successes in $n$ trials? :: Beta$(a + x, b + n - x)$.

! recap | One-minute revision
- Sign test: binomial with $p = \frac{1}{2}$. Signed-rank: needs symmetry. Mann-Whitney: $E[U] = \frac{mn}{2}$.
- $r_s = 1 - \frac{6\sum d^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}$; Kendall's tau counts concordant minus discordant pairs.
- Posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior; squared error loss gives the posterior mean.
